The figure 8 the Sun draws over Hilo

Photograph the Sun from the same spot at the same clock time every day for a year, and the dots trace a lopsided figure 8 called the analemma. Its height comes from the seasons; its width comes from the same two orbital effects that shift sunrise and sunset.

Why clock time, not solar noon

At true solar noon the Sun is always exactly on the north–south line, so a year of solar-noon photos would make a straight vertical stripe. The figure 8 appears when you hold the clock fixed. Some days the Sun reaches the meridian before 12:20 and has drifted west by the time you shoot; other days it hasn't arrived yet and still sits to the east. That east–west scatter is the equation of time turned into an angle: each minute is a quarter of a degree.

Why it's an 8

Up and down, once a year

The tilt of Earth's axis carries the Sun north of the equator in June and south of it in December. That's one full swing per year, and it sets the height of the figure.

Side to side, twice a year

The tilt's effect on timing peaks four times a year, swinging east, west, east, west. Two side-to-side cycles against one up-down cycle trace a loop at the top and a loop at the bottom, crossing in the middle in mid-April and late August.

Why the loops are lopsided

Earth's elliptical orbit adds a second side-to-side push that cycles once a year. Earth moves fastest at perihelion in early January, and the offset that builds up is largest a season later, in early April and early October. On its own it would turn the figure into a single tilted oval; press "Orbit only" to see it. In late October and mid-February it adds to the tilt effect, swelling the bottom loop. In mid-May and late July it works against the tilt effect, pinching the top loop. That's why every analemma has a fat loop from late August to mid-April and a thin one from mid-April to late August.

Hilo's twist

Hilo sits south of the Tropic of Cancer, so the top of its analemma pokes past straight overhead. Twice a year, around late May and late July (circled on the figure), the noon Sun is at the zenith and upright objects cast almost no shadow. In Hawaiʻi this is called Lāhainā Noon. Between those dates the summer loop sits on the north side of the sky.
