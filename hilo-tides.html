Why Hilo's tides rise and fall the way they do

The Moon and Sun stretch the ocean into two bulges, and Earth turns Hilo through them about twice a day. Move through the next 30 days to see how their positions set each high and low, and why the two daily highs are rarely the same height.

Two bulges, about two tides a day

The Moon pulls hardest on the ocean facing it and weakest on the far side. Relative to Earth's center, the near water is pulled toward the Moon and the far water is left behind, so the ocean stretches into two bulges on opposite sides. As Earth turns, Hilo passes through both. The ocean can't keep up with the turning Earth, so at Hilo a high tide arrives on average about 2¾ hours after the Moon passes overhead or underfoot. The Moon also moves along its orbit, so Hilo needs about 24 hours 50 minutes to come back under it. That's why high tide arrives roughly 50 minutes later each day.

Spring and neap tides

The Sun raises its own pair of bulges, a bit less than half the size of the Moon's. At new and full moon the two sets line up and add, giving the larger spring tides. At first and last quarter they sit at right angles and partly cancel, giving the smaller neap tides. The 30-day chart shows the range swelling and shrinking on that two-week rhythm.

Why Hilo's two highs are unequal

The Moon swings north and south

Over each month the Moon moves from about 28° north of the equator to 28° south and back. When it's well north or south, the bulges tilt with it, as in the side view. Hilo passes close to the center of one bulge but only grazes the other, so one high tide is clearly higher than the other.

Hawaiʻi's mixed tide

Many places have nearly equal highs. In Hawaiʻi the once-a-day part of the tide is unusually strong compared with the twice-a-day part, so the inequality is large. Hilo's tide is called mixed semidiurnal: two highs and two lows a day, often with one pair much weaker than the other, and sometimes a nearly flat stretch where a high and low almost merge.

When the Moon crosses the equator, roughly every two weeks, the bulges straighten and the two highs come out nearly equal again.

Why the range is small

On an average day Hilo's tide moves only about 2.4 feet between the higher high and the lower low. Real tides aren't simple bulges; they move as huge waves that slosh around each ocean basin, pivoting around points of near-zero tide. Hawaiʻi sits in the middle of the Pacific, far from the continental shelves that pile tides up, so its tides stay modest compared with places like the Pacific Northwest.
