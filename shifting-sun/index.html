Why noon drifts: two orbital effects

Elliptical orbit

Earth moves fastest at perihelion in early January. Each day it has to turn a little farther to bring the Sun back overhead, so solar days run long. The lag swings about ±7.5 minutes once a year.

Axial tilt

The axis keeps pointing the same way in space. Near the solstices the Sun's apparent path runs parallel to the equator, so it gains more eastward ground per day than at the equinoxes. That swings about ±10 minutes, twice a year.

In late December both effects push solar noon later by about 30 seconds a day, while day length barely changes near the solstice. North of the tropics, sunrise keeps getting later for weeks after the shortest day, which is why the latest sunrise lands in January rather than on December 21. In June the tilt effect alone pulls the earliest sunrise before the solstice and the latest sunset after it. These effects come from Earth's orbit, so they're the same everywhere on the planet.

Why clock time, not solar noon

At true solar noon the Sun is always exactly on the north–south line, so a year of solar-noon photos would make a straight vertical stripe. The figure 8 appears when you hold the clock fixed. Some days the Sun reaches the meridian before the photo is taken and has drifted west by then; other days it hasn't arrived yet and still sits to the east. That east–west scatter is the equation of time turned into an angle: each minute is a quarter of a degree.

Why it's an 8

Up and down, once a year

The tilt of Earth's axis carries the Sun north of the equator in June and south of it in December. That's one full swing per year, and it sets the height of the figure.

Side to side, twice a year

The tilt's effect on timing peaks four times a year, swinging east, west, east, west. Two side-to-side cycles against one up-down cycle trace a loop at each end, crossing in the middle in mid-April and late August.

Why the loops are lopsided

Earth's elliptical orbit adds a second side-to-side push that cycles once a year. Earth moves fastest at perihelion in early January, and the offset that builds up is largest a season later, in early April and early October. On its own it would turn the figure into a single tilted oval; press "Orbit only" to see it. In late October and mid-February it adds to the tilt effect, swelling the December loop. In mid-May and late July it works against the tilt effect, pinching the June loop. That's why every analemma has a fat loop from late August to mid-April and a thin one from mid-April to late August.
