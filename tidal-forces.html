Stretch, not pull

The Moon pulls the near side of Earth a little harder than the center, and the center a little harder than the far side. Subtract the pull on Earth's center and what's left points outward on both the near and far sides and inward around the middle. The ocean flows toward the two outward-pointing zones and piles into bulges.

Why the Moon beats the Sun

The Sun's gravity on Earth is about 180 times stronger than the Moon's, but tides depend on how fast gravity changes across Earth's width. That falls off with the cube of distance, not the square. The Sun is 390 times farther away, so its tidal effect ends up only about 46% of the Moon's.

Because of that cube, distance matters a lot. The Moon's elliptical orbit brings it about 10% closer at perigee than at apogee, which makes its tidal pull about 40% stronger. Earth is closest to the Sun in early January, adding about 5% to the Sun's share. When a full or new moon lands near perigee you get perigean spring tides, the "king tides" that flood low coastal roads.

Friction carries the bulge forward

Earth turns once a day; the Moon takes 27 days to go around. The ocean and seafloor are dragged along with the spin, and water takes time to respond, so the bulge ends up carried a little ahead of the line to the Moon. The Moon's gravity tugs that leading bulge backward, like a hand on a spinning wheel. Most of the energy is lost as heat in shallow seas, where tidal currents scrub the seafloor.

The Moon gets a forward push

The pull works both ways. The leading bulge is closer to the Moon than the trailing one, so it tugs the Moon slightly forward along its orbit. That forward push adds energy, and an orbit with more energy is a bigger one. The Moon spirals outward about 3.8 cm a year, a rate measured by bouncing lasers off reflectors left on the Moon by Apollo astronauts.

Earth's lost spin doesn't vanish; it's handed to the Moon's orbit, and the total angular momentum stays the same. There's a twist: pushing the Moon forward makes it move slower. A wider orbit is a slower one, so the month stretches out even as the Moon gains energy. The real lead angle is only a few degrees and the open-ocean tide is less than a meter high; the model exaggerates both so you can see the forces.

3 · Deep time

Shorter days in the past

Evidence-

Corals and clams lay down growth bands every day and thicken them with the seasons, so counting bands per year counts days per year. The year itself hasn't changed length, so more days means shorter days. Layered tidal sediments do the same job for tides and months. Together they show days of about 22 hours some 400 to 600 million years ago, with the Moon a few percent closer.

The braking hasn't been steady. Today's ocean basins happen to amplify tides, so the Moon is retreating faster now than its long-term average; if tidal friction had always been as strong as it is today, the Moon would have been almost touching Earth only about 1.5 billion years ago, because the braking grows steeply as the Moon gets closer. The rock record rules that out. In the far future the process would end with Earth's day matching the month, but that would take far longer than the Sun will last.
